Estimating hitter contact ability

A simple model estimates a hitter’s chance of making contact when they swing, adjusting for pitch context (IZ/OOZ, FB/non-FB, batting side, and throwing side) and opponent quality. Pooling information across contexts and shrinking player effects toward the population mean stabilize estimates for limited samples. Adding batter and pitcher effects improved held-out probability predictions, while uncertainty intervals describe how precisely ability is estimated.

2. The supplied distribution is the modeling target

The data show extreme platoon differences: in-zone contact is about 45–50% in same-side matchups and 88–90% in opposite-side matchups. The pattern also appears out of zone. I treated these outcomes as ground truth rather than correcting them toward customary baseball expectations.

Those differences raise substantial concerns about representativeness. The estimates describe this supplied player and pitch distribution; their calibration should not be assumed to transfer to actual MLB play. Each batter has one observed batting side, so switch-hitting is absent from this dataset. Conditional pitch independence is also an imperfect assumption: without game, plate-appearance, or date information, the dependence structure is difficult to address.

3. A regularized logistic model with shared player effects

On swung-at pitches, contact outcomes follow a Bernoulli likelihood with probability:

P(contact | swing) = logistic(αcontext + ubatter + vpitcher)

Context means IZ/OOZ, FB/non-FB, batting side, and throwing side. The population component contains all 16 combinations of these features. Jointly estimated batter and pitcher intercepts move predictions above or below that context baseline. A pitcher who allows more contact than expected raises the predicted contact rate for the same hitter and context.

Gaussian penalties regress sparse player effects toward the mean. Player intercepts are shared across contexts, so non-fastball observations inform fastball estimates and observations against RHP inform LHP estimates. Context logits have N(0, 2.5²) priors; player effects have N(0, 1/precision) priors. The context-prior standard deviation σ = 2.5 was chosen arbitrarily for this first pass, rather than tuned. It should be treated as a tunable hyperparameter in future iterations. A mode fit and Laplace approximation provide a modest Bayesian interpretation without MCMC.

Validation evaluates batter and pitcher precisions from 0.01 to 100. Among settings within 0.001 log loss of the best score, the rule selects the smallest summed prior variance, favoring stronger shrinkage when performance is nearly equivalent. Both selected precisions are 1. Player effects are centered within handedness for neutral-opponent interpretation.

4. Held-out performance and calibration

A 60/20/20 split of swings is stratified by the 16 contexts and retains every batter and pitcher in each partition: 29,682 training, 9,895 validation, and 9,895 test swings. After selection, models are refit on training plus validation. Test swings remain outside fitting, tuning, and reference weights.

Log loss, Brier score, and AUC (shown in the table but not the plots) improve as context and player information are added. The final model’s mean test prediction is 60.6%, versus 61.0% observed contact. Assessment also covers all 16 contexts, marginal splits, player sample-size groups, and batter-balanced scores; the largest absolute context-level mean error is 5.3 percentage points.

A pair-grouped development check keeps each pitcher–batter combination entirely within one partition. Its final-model log loss is 0.453, compared with 0.446 in the primary test. This supports robustness to the split choice, but is a sensitivity check: shrinkage selection already used development outcomes. Without dates or game identifiers, both designs assess held-out swings involving known players, rather than validated future-season forecasts or game-independent performance.

5. What the uncertainty measures cover

A local Gaussian approximation around the fitted posterior mode supplies joint coefficient draws, preserving covariance among context, batter, and pitcher coefficients. Each draw is transformed into probabilities and averaged over the fixed reference mixture. The reported bounds are approximate 95% credible intervals conditional on the chosen model and shrinkage settings.

Source of uncertaintyTreatment in this analysis
Parameter uncertaintyIncluded through joint coefficient draws, reflecting finite information under the specified model.
Bernoulli outcome randomnessIncluded in replicated contact counts and predictive checks, rather than in intervals for the underlying probability alone.
Model and selection uncertaintyArchitecture, selected penalties, and reference weights are held fixed. Misspecification and uncertainty in choosing them are not propagated.
Measurement and sampling uncertaintyLabel/feature error, nonrepresentative sampling, and changing future pitch selection are not separately modeled. Finite-sample parameter uncertainty is included only under the model assumptions.

A recovery simulation using the development matchup structure and selected Gaussian scales covered 95.5% of evaluated player targets across 20 replications. This supports the approximation under the assumed hierarchy, but does not establish coverage under distribution shift, pitch dependence, or model-selection uncertainty.

6. Additional information desired

To support and improve this analysis, I would want the following additional information:

  • Pitch traits and count: velocity, movement/break, release, pitch location, and count would let adjustment reflect pitch quality more directly, rather than relying on pitcher identity as a proxy.
  • Dates and game identifiers: these would support chronological validation for future projections and splits that keep each game’s observations together.
  • Larger, more representative samples: broader coverage would help investigate the unusual platoon patterns, reduce concerns about sampling bias, and support more sophisticated models before transferring estimates to MLB.

7. Future improvements

The following changes could improve the modeling approach as more data and validation become available:

  • Tune the context-prior scale: treat σ as a hyperparameter selected using training and validation data, rather than retaining the arbitrary value of 2.5. Keep the test set outside that selection.
  • Player-specific context effects: add partially pooled individual platoon, fastball, or zone responses so that players can depart from the shared population patterns without abandoning mean regression.
  • Richer models when justified: with more data and features, evaluate a neural network with a KL-divergence objective, similar to my previous work with the Pirates, against this simpler baseline. For fixed targets, KL divergence and cross-entropy differ only by a target-dependent constant; the current binary setup therefore gains no new objective. However, moving to non-binary probability targets could be beneficial as other probabilistic models are integrated into the model stack, with KL divergence providing a natural measure of agreement between target and predicted distributions.
  • Forecast validation: with date and game information, evaluate future periods using chronological splits and respect game-level dependence.
  • A swing model: integrate a model that predicts P(swing) to distinguish pitch selection from contact ability and support changing future swing-context mixtures.

8. Another question to explore

How much of the observed contact-rate variation reflects the pitches hitters choose to swing at, versus their contact ability on comparable pitches?

A joint swing/contact analysis with richer pitch features would help investigate that distinction, separating differences in swing selection from differences in execution against similar pitches.
